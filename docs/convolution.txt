What do blurring an image, adding an
echo to music, detecting edges in a
photograph, and teaching artificial
intelligence to recognize objects have
in common? They all involve a
surprisingly powerful mathematical
operation. Convolution. At first,
convolution looks intimidating. An
integral stretching from negative
infinity to positive infinity. A
mysterious function that gets flipped,
shifted, multiplied, and integrated. But
here's the interesting part. Once you
understand the geometry behind
convolution, the formula starts to make
sense. You'll discover that convolution
is fundamentally about one simple
question. How much does one function
overlap with another as we move it
across a signal? That single idea
connects calculus, signal processing,
image processing, differential
equations, probability, and artificial
intelligence. In this video, we're going
to build convolution from scratch. We'll
begin with a simple visual experiment.
Then we'll derive the mathematical
formula, understand why one function
gets flipped, and see how convolution
works with actual numbers. Finally,
we'll explore why convolution is so
important in modern technology. Let's
begin. Imagine that you're measuring the
intensity of a signal over time. For
example, suppose a sensor detects a
sudden burst of light. Before the burst,
the intensity is zero. During the burst,
the intensity becomes one. After the
burst, it returns to zero. We can
represent this using a mathematical
function. Let's call it f of t. For
simplicity, f of t equals 1 when t lies
between 0 and 1 and zero everywhere
else. This is called a rectangular
pulse. It is one of the simplest signals
we can study. Now imagine introducing
another function. Let's call it g of t.
This function acts like a moving window
that examines different parts of the
original signal. Think of f as the
signal we have and g as the window that
moves across it. As we move the window,
the amount of overlap changes and that
changing overlap will become our output.
This is the central geometric intuition
behind convolution. But how do we
convert the amount of overlap into a
mathematical function? To answer that,
we need to understand a more fundamental
idea. Multiplication. Suppose two
functions are defined along the same
horizontal axis. At every position, each
function has a particular value. We can
multiply those values together. If
either function is zero, their product
is zero. If both functions are positive,
their product is positive. So the
product tells us where both functions
contribute simultaneously. Let's see
what happens with our rectangular
pulses. Suppose both functions have a
height of one. Where they don't overlap,
their product is zero. Where they
overlap, their product is one. Now we
have a new function, the product of the
two functions. But this product still
tells us what happens at individual
positions. What if we want a single
number representing the total overlap?
We integrate. Integration adds up the
contributions across the horizontal
axis. For our rectangular pulses,
integration simply measures the length
of the overlapping interval. If the
overlap has length zero, the integral is
zero. If the overlap has length 1/2, the
integral is 1/2. If the overlap has
length one, the integral is one. This
gives us an important principle.
Multiply the functions point by point,
then integrate the product. The result
measures their combined contribution
across the domain. But convolution
introduces one additional ingredient
movement. Instead of comparing two
functions at just one fixed position, we
move one function across the other. At
every position, we calculate the overlap
again that produces a new function and
that function is the convolution. Let's
make the idea visual. Our original
signal remains fixed. A second function
moves across it. Initially, the moving
function is far away from the signal.
There is no overlap. So the output is
zero. Now the moving function begins
entering the signal. The overlapping
region becomes larger. The output
increases. When the moving function
completely covers the signal, the
overlap reaches its maximum. The output
becomes largest. Then the moving
function starts leaving the signal. The
overlap becomes smaller. The output
decreases. Finally, the moving function
moves completely beyond the signal. The
overlap disappears and the output
returns to zero. Notice something
remarkable. Our original signal was a
rectangular pulse, but the output has a
different shape. The output was created
entirely by moving a second function
across the first. This is the basic
behavior of convolution. It transforms
two functions into a third function. We
can represent this operation
symbolically as f convolved with g. The
asterisk represents convolution, not
ordinary multiplication. Instead, f
convolved with g describes the function
produced by sliding one function across
the other and calculating their combined
contribution at every position. With
different functions, we can produce
entirely different outputs. A Gaussian
window may smooth a signal. A specially
designed kernel may emphasize rapid
changes. Another kernel may reveal
repeated patterns. The result depends on
the shapes of the two functions. Now we
understand the intuition. But
mathematics has a detail that often
confuses beginners. In the actual
convolution formula, the moving function
is reflected before it is shifted. Why?
Let's derive the formula and investigate
that question. The continuous
convolution of two functions f and g is
defined by the integral from negative
infinity to positive infinity of f of
toao multiplied by g of t minus toao
with respect to toao. Let's understand
every part of this expression. First we
have f of toao. This represents the
original function evaluated at a
position called toao. The symbol toao is
simply a variable of integration. We use
it instead of t because t represents the
location where we want to calculate the
output. Next we have g of t minus toao.
This is the moving function. Its
argument contains t minus toao. That
subtraction is essential. As we change
t, the relative position of the two
functions changes. Now we multiply f of
toao by g of t minus toao. This
calculates the product of the two
functions at corresponding positions.
Finally, we integrate over all values of
toao. That adds together the
contributions from the entire domain.
The result is one number for each value
of t. As t changes, that number changes
too. Therefore, the output is itself a
function of t. The integration limits
extend from negative infinity to
positive infinity. In practice, if the
functions are zero outside a finite
interval, we only need to integrate over
the region where their product can be
non zero. Now, let's address the
question. Why do we see t minus toao
instead of simply adding the variables
together? The answer comes from the
geometry of shifting a function.
Consider a function that is not
symmetric. Its left side looks different
from its right side. Now reflected
across the vertical axis. The left and
right sides exchange positions. This is
called reflection or time reversal. For
a function g of t, its reflected version
is g of negative t. Now shift the
reflected function by an amount t. The
result can be written as g of t minus
toao. When viewed as a function of toao,
as t changes, the reflected function
moves across the original signal. At
each position, we multiply the two
functions and integrate. This is
convolution. The reflection is not an
accidental trick. It is part of the
mathematical definition of convolution.
Here's a subtle but important
distinction. If we slide a function
without reflecting it, we are
calculating a correlation-like operation
depending on the convention being used.
If we reflect the function and then
slide it, we obtain convolution. For
symmetric functions such as a centered
Gaussian, the reflection makes no
visible difference because the function
looks identical after flipping. But for
asymmetric functions, the distinction
matters. This is why the flip can seem
invisible in some visual explanations.
To really understand convolution,
remember four conceptual steps. Flip,
shift, multiply, integrate. Now let's
work through an exact numerical example.
Let's define two functions. Our first
function f of t equals 1 when t lies
between 0 and 1 and zero everywhere
else. Our second function g of t is
exactly the same. We want to calculate
their convolution using the convolution
formula. We multiply f of toao by g of t
minus toao and integrate with respect to
toao. Since both functions are
rectangular pulses, their product is one
wherever the two intervals overlap and
zero everywhere else. So our problem
becomes surprisingly simple. We need to
find the length of the overlap between
two intervals. Let's examine three
cases. First, suppose t is less than
zero. The two pulses do not overlap.
Therefore, their product is zero
everywhere. The integral is zero. So the
convolution is zero whenever t is less
than zero. Now consider the second case.
Suppose t lies between 0 and 1. The
pulses overlap partially. The overlap
interval has length t. For example, when
t equals 0.25,
the overlap length is 0.25.
When t= 0.6, the overlap length is 0.6.
Therefore, the convolution output
increases linearly. In this interval,
the convolution equals t. Now consider
the third case. Suppose t lies between 1
and 2. The moving pulse gradually leaves
the fixed pulse. The overlap length
decreases. The overlap is 2 minus t.
When t = 1, the overlap length is 1.
When t= 1.5, the overlap length is 0.5.
When t equals 2, the overlap disappears.
Therefore, in this interval, the
convolution equals 2 minus t. For t
greater than 2, the pulses no longer
overlap. The output returns to zero.
Putting everything together, we obtain a
peace-wise function. The convolution
equals 0 before zero. It equals t
between 0 and 1. It equals 2 minus t
between 1 and 2 and it equals 0 after 2.
Look at the result. We started with two
rectangular functions. Their convolution
is a triangular function. That triangle
is not an approximation. It follows
exactly from the definition. Two simple
rectangular pulses combine to produce a
new function that rises linearly and
then falls linearly. This is one of the
clearest examples of how convolution
changes the shape of a signal. And
notice something else. Each original
pulse has a width of one. The resulting
triangle extends from 0 to two. Its
support has a width of two. Convolution
combines the widths of the two intervals
in this example. This behavior becomes
useful when studying signals,
probability distributions, and many
other mathematical objects. But why does
convolution appear in so many different
fields? To answer that, we need to
connect it to real systems. Imagine a
physical system. You provide an input.
The system responds with an output. For
example, you clap in an empty room. The
clap is the input. The room produces an
echoing sound that fades over time. The
output depends not only on the original
clap, but also on the characteristics of
the room. A large hall may produce a
long reverberation. A small room with
soundabsorbing walls may produce a much
shorter response. Now imagine that we
know how the room responds to a very
short impulse. We call this response the
impulse response. Let's denote it by h
of t. For a linear time invariant
system, the output y of t can be
calculated by convolving the input x of
t with the impulse response h of t. In
other words, the output equals the
integral of x of multiplied by h of t
minus toao integrated over all values of
toao. This is one of the most important
applications of convolution. Why does it
work? Imagine breaking a complicated
input signal into many tiny impulses.
Each impulse produces a scaled and
shifted copy of the systems impulse
response. Because the system is linear,
we can add all these individual
responses together. Convolution performs
exactly that weighted addition. Every
part of the input contributes to the
output according to the systems
response. This explains why convolution
is so useful. Instead of solving the
entire system from scratch for every
possible input, we can characterize the
system using its impulse response. Once
we know that response, convolution lets
us calculate the output for many
different inputs. This principle is
widely used in audio processing,
electronics, communications, and control
systems. But the same mathematical
operation also appears in image
processing. And there the moving window
becomes a small matrix. An image is a
two-dimensional grid of pixels. Each
pixel stores information such as
brightness or color. To process an
image, we can use a small matrix called
a kernel or filter. For example,
consider a 3x3 averaging kernel. Every
entry in this matrix has the value 1
9th. Now place this kernel over a small
region of the image. Multiply each pixel
by the corresponding kernel weight. Add
the nine products. The result becomes
the new value of the pixel at the chosen
output location. Then move the kernel to
the next position. Repeat the process
across the image. This is a discrete
two-dimensional convolution-like
filtering operation. There is a
technical detail worth knowing. In many
image processing libraries and
convolutional neural network
implementations, the operation called
convolution is actually crossorrelation.
The kernel is applied without flipping
it. For symmetric kernels such as the
averaging kernel we just used, flipping
makes no difference. For asymmetric
kernels, the distinction matters. Now,
what does this averaging kernel
accomplish? It replaces each pixel with
a local weighted average. Large
differences between neighboring pixels
tend to become smaller. The image
becomes smoother. Fine details may
disappear. Noise may be reduced. The
result is a blur. But we can do much
more than blur an image. Consider a
kernel that compares pixel values on the
left with pixel values on the right.
Such a kernel can respond strongly to
changes across the image and highlight
vertical edges. A different kernel can
detect horizontal edges. Other kernels
can sharpen an image, emphasize
textures, or identify specific local
patterns. The principle is the same.
Choose a kernel, slide it across the
image, multiply corresponding values,
add the results, repeat. A tiny matrix
can transform an entire image. And this
brings us to one of the most important
applications of convolution in modern
artificial intelligence. How can a
computer recognize a cat in a
photograph? The image may contain
millions of visual details. There may be
different backgrounds, lighting

training, the network adjusts these
weights to reduce its prediction errors.
Over time, different filters can become

Some filters may respond strongly to

idea is that the same learned filter can
be applied at many different positions.
The network does not need a completely

computer vision and many other machine

connection is this. The operation we
first understood by sliding a simple
window over a graph can also help a
